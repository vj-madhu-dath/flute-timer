Three ways to use a play/rest log

A log is only useful if it helps you notice something. These are three low-pressure ways to look, not three new targets to hit.

14 October 2026 5 min read Flute Timer notes

The first time you finish a session with Flute Timer, the laps look like a receipt: a column of play and rest, a total at the bottom. It is tempting to treat that receipt as a verdict. Resist that. The log is a description of time, not an evaluation of the practice.

What follows are three ways to use it. They are deliberately modest. None of them requires a spreadsheet, and none of them asks you to chase a perfect balance.

1. Give a warm-up a shape

Start the timer before your first long tone. Play the warm-up you already use — slow scales, intervals, harmonics, a few phrases from a piece — and leave the timer running. When you stop, look at the play laps before you look at the totals.

What you are looking for is not a number. It is the shape. Are there many short play laps with tiny gaps? That might be the natural rhythm of long tones: play, breathe, reset, play. Are there long unbroken stretches? Perhaps the warm-up turned into a run-through without your noticing.

Then ask one question: did the warm-up prepare the next thing, or did it become the whole session? Either answer is allowed. The point is that the log makes the transition visible.

A simple version

Play a slow scale for three to four minutes. Rest between each phrase for as long as feels natural. When you finish, glance at the bar: the play and rest colors should show the rhythm you felt. If they do not, the timer may need calibration, not you.

2. Loop a passage, rest between attempts

Choose four bars that are not yet comfortable. Play them once, stop, and take a breath. Then play them again. Do this four or five times without touching the app.

The log will show a series of short play laps separated by rest. That is exactly what a deliberate loop looks like. The useful information comes later: compare the length of the play laps. If they shrink with each repetition, you may be speeding up; if they grow, you may be adding notes or losing the boundary of the passage.

You can also use the log to protect the rest. It is easy to tell yourself that the pause is “just a second” and then launch into the next attempt while the previous one is still ringing in your ears. Seeing a rest lap on screen makes the pause a real part of the work.

Try not to fix the loop to a target ratio. A slow, difficult passage may need more rest than a fluent one. The appropriate balance is the one that lets you hear what you are doing.

3. Do a weekly audit

Once a week — or once a month, if that is more honest — use the timer for a normal session and look at the overall shape afterward. This is not an examination. It is a quiet look at how you actually spend your time.

Three questions are enough:

  1. What took the most time? Warm-up, repertoire, a single difficult passage, or something else?
  2. Where did the rest go? Was it scattered in small gaps, or gathered after longer play laps?
  3. What surprised me? The answer is often small: “I thought I practiced the second movement for twenty minutes; the lap says eight.”

Write one sentence in a notebook. Not a score, not a goal, just an observation. Over a few weeks, the sentences may become more useful than any single session's totals.

Keep the scale small

A ten- or fifteen-minute sample is plenty for an audit. A log from a short, ordinary session is usually more useful than a heroic one you would not repeat.

What not to do

Do not compare your ratio with another player's; their session, instrument, room, and goals are different. Do not treat a high rest percentage as wisdom or a low one as discipline. Do not use the timer to push through pain, fatigue, or an embouchure that needs a break.

And do not confuse the log with the practice. The timer can tell you that you spent eleven minutes playing and four minutes resting. It cannot tell you whether either was worth doing. That judgment belongs to your ears, your teacher, and the part of you that notices when something finally clicks.

Used this way, the log is not a scoreboard. It is a small mirror held up to the time you already spent — and sometimes that is enough to change the next session.

Start with an ordinary session. The log is most useful when you forget it is there. Open Flute Timer, play as usual, and look at the shape afterward.
